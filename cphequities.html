𝗚𝗢𝗜𝗡𝗚 𝗔𝗟𝗟-𝗜𝗡 𝗢𝗡 𝗔𝗜? 𝗧𝗛𝗘 𝗠𝗔𝗧𝗛 𝗦𝗔𝗬𝗦 "𝗡𝗢" After my post about Leopold Aschenbrenner a few weeks ago, I was asked a fair question: If I believe strongly in AI infrastructure, why not go all-in? Why worry about volatility at all, when my time horizon is long? It turns out there is a mathematical answer to this question. And it was discovered almost 70 years ago by a chain-smoking physicist from Texas. John Kelly was a WWII pilot who earned a PhD in physics and later worked at the legendary Bell Labs in the 1950s, alongside Claude Shannon, the father of information theory. In 1956, Kelly published a paper that answered a deceptively simple question: If you have an edge in a bet, 𝗵𝗼𝘄 𝗺𝘂𝗰𝗵 𝗼𝗳 𝘆𝗼𝘂𝗿 𝗺𝗼𝗻𝗲𝘆 𝘀𝗵𝗼𝘂𝗹𝗱 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝗽𝘂𝘁 𝗼𝗻 𝗶𝘁? His formula became known as the Kelly criterion. It has been used by professional gamblers and by the mathematician Ed Thorp to beat both blackjack and Wall Street. Here is the idea, in its simplest form. Imagine a coin that lands your way 60% of the time. When you win, you double your bet. When you lose, your bet is gone. This is a fantastic game – you have a clear edge, and over time you should make good money. Now, imagine going all-in on every flip. You win the first flip and double your money. You win the second and double again. But at some point, the coin lands against you. And when it does, you lose everything. The edge was real, but the ruin was guaranteed anyway. Kelly's formula tells you the optimal amount to bet in this game: 20% of your money on each flip. Bet less, and you leave returns on the table. Bet more, and the swings become so violent that they destroy your compounding – even though you keep having the edge. This is the part most investors never truly understand. 𝗕𝗲𝘁𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘁𝗼𝗼 𝗺𝘂𝗰𝗵 𝗼𝗻 𝗮 𝘄𝗶𝗻𝗻𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗯𝗲𝘁 𝗰𝗮𝗻 𝗹𝗼𝘀𝗲 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝗺𝗼𝗻𝗲𝘆. If your portfolio drops 50%, you need a 100% gain just to get back to where you started. Volatility is not just an uncomfortable feeling on red days. It is a tax on your compounding. This is why the answer to "why not go all-in" is not about nerves or caution. It is about mathematics. And there is one more thing. Kelly's formula assumes you know your odds precisely. In a casino, you do. In the stock market, you never do – you only have estimates, and estimates can be wrong. This is why professional investors who use Kelly typically bet half of what the formula suggests, or even less. The more uncertain your edge, the smaller your bet should be. Aschenbrenner had a real edge. His AI thesis was correct. But with leverage of nearly 400%, he was betting multiples of what Kelly would ever allow, and the mathematics caught up with him in a few brutal weeks in July. My own portfolio reflects the same conviction he had. AI infrastructure is my biggest theme, with positions like $MU (Micron Technology, Inc.), $LRCX (Lam Research Corp), and $TSM (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co Ltd - ADR). The position is large because my conviction is high – that is Kelly thinking. But it is not all-in. The healthcare and defensive positions with low correlation to the AI trade are not a lack of conviction.
