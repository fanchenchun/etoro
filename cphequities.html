In 2021, the researcher Joseph Carlsmith built a model that is still widely used to think about AI risk. For a power-seeking AI scenario to cause an existential catastrophe, a whole chain of events has to happen: advanced AI has to be built, there have to be strong incentives to deploy it, it has to be harder to build safe AI than unsafe AI, some systems have to seek power, humanity has to lose control permanently, and it all has to end in catastrophe. Every link in the chain has to hold. And as probabilities are multiplied, one unlikely link pulls the whole risk down. Carlsmith's own estimate was around 5% by 2070, which he later raised to above 10%. That is a serious number, but note the timeline. For the 2030 scenario to play out, all six conditions would have to be met within the next four years. In 2023, the think tank AI Impacts surveyed 2,778 AI researchers, and up to half of them put at least a 10% chance on human extinction or permanent loss of control at some point in the future. At the same time, 68% believed good outcomes from AI were more likely than bad ones. So optimism and concern are not opposites. And as investors, we know this feeling well. You can be bullish on a trend and still respect the tail risk. The most useful counterweight comes from RAND, the American research institution. They asked a very practical question: how would an AI physically kill every human being? They looked at nuclear war, biological weapons, and climate manipulation, and the conclusion was that it is far harder than it sounds. No single nuclear button can be hacked. A pandemic or a climate catastrophe would require control of laboratories, factories, raw materials, and supply chains. So, intelligence can develop fast. Factories, energy supplies, and logistics cannot. Actions in the physical world take time and leave traces, which gives humans a chance to notice and intervene. And this is the strongest argument against the 2030 scenario. This does not mean we can relax.
